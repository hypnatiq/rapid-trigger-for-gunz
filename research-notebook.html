03

My everyday profile

Keeping my keyboard as a keyboard

What I wanted from the everyday profile

  • I wanted one profile I could trust outside GunZ.
    • It still needed to feel normal for work, daily use, and 100+ WPM typing.
    • I did not want keys triggering from every small resting-pressure change.
  • I did not want one game to decide how the entire keyboard felt.
    • The everyday profile stays available while the GunZ profile carries the more focused movement testing.

What Rapid Trigger adds for me in GunZ

  • RT D shortens the release travel I need between the two taps of a dash.
    • It changes how much I lift before the keyboard recognizes that the first tap is no longer held.
    • It does not remove every source of input, game, network, or server delay.
  • RT A changes the re-press travel I need for the next tap.
    • It begins after my key travel changes from lifting to pressing.
    • It lets me tune repeated movement without lowering first actuation only to chase speed.

My wording, fact-checked: RT D reduces required release travel. RT A reduces or increases required re-press travel. Neither setting promises a fixed time saving.

My GunZ-specific profile

A separate choice for more intentional movement

Why I keep a separate GunZ profile

  • I can test movement without losing the profile I trust for everything else.
    • I duplicate a working profile before changing actuation, RT A, or RT D.
    • If a test gets worse, I can return to the saved version instead of guessing my way back.
  • The separate profile gives the experiment a clear purpose.
    • I use it for cleaner, more intentional movement in GunZ.
    • I keep the same in-game setup I already used, including my keys, sensitivity, resolution, FOV, camera distance, crosshair, and client.
    • I build the keyboard base with actuation, RT D, and RT A only. I do not add SOCD, DKS, Mod Tap, analog movement, or another Hall Effect feature while I am judging those three settings.
  • It is still a personal choice, not a Rapid Trigger requirement.
    • The public guide explains the method without asking anyone to copy my numbers.
    • The notebook shows only the current Wootility checkpoint so the earlier profile screenshots are not mistaken for the active result.
05

What RT D and RT A change in my GunZ movement

GunZ symptom first · technical behavior second

When my late-dash return does not clear

  • What I notice in GunZ: the opening dash is normal, but the return dash is repeatedly not silent.
    • I treat the return as its own two-tap input.
    • I wait for the same problem to repeat before changing a value.
  • Why I check : it controls how much I lift before the keyboard releases the first tap.
    • If RT D is too high, the first tap can remain held longer than my movement rhythm expects.
    • If RT D is too low, a small pressure change can release a direction before I intend it.

When the next dash breaks my shot rhythm

  • What I notice in GunZ: release is clean, but the next dash no longer lines up with the firing moment I expect during slash shot.
    • The character is not moving at a slower base speed.
    • The later tap is arriving too late in my movement-and-aim sequence.
  • Why I check : it controls how much I press again before the keyboard recognizes the next tap.
    • If RT A is too high, the next tap can arrive late or fail before my key travel changes direction again.
    • If RT A is too low, a small bounce or unfinished motion can create an early repeat.

The part I had to correct: RT A does not directly set GunZ speed. It sets how far I must re-press the key. My finger motion determines how much real time passes before the keyboard recognizes the next tap.

06

How I reached my current method

What changed my thinking and the sequence I can repeat

What changed my thinking

  • One successful move was not enough.
    • At 1.50 mm actuation, 1.50 mm RT A, and 0.55 mm RT D, I could perform one selected move from standstill but could not continue the movement chain.
    • That taught me to judge the release and re-press separately instead of treating the first success as a finished tune.
  • Training and real matches answered different questions.
    • Training showed whether the movement and late dash could repeat on their own.
    • Clan War was my main real-match validation because it is my preferred, higher-skill mode and gives me less room for errors. Team Deathmatch gave me another full-map validation of movement, character control, aiming, and enemy pace.
    • I did not use Hall Duel as the only final proof because most fights there stay mainly left and right.
  • Consistency became my speed limit.
    • The smallest value was not automatically the best value.
    • If the keyboard accepted an input before I intended it, I could get a dead dash, stand still, tumble, or break the movement chain.
    • I stopped when movement stayed clean, repeatable, and intentional.
  • I kept the keyboard tune separate from sensitivity.
    • I held my mouse, sensitivity, resolution, FOV, camera distance, crosshair, and client steady while testing actuation, RT D, and RT A.
    • I only reconsider aim after the movement itself passed in real fights, and I may not need to change sensitivity at all.

What this became: the method below follows the same order that changed my thinking, from one controlled movement to training, full-map matches, and a saved long-term checkpoint.

The method I use now

This is the full sequence I can repeat without separating the lesson from the steps it created.

GunZ goal

Keep the late dash clean while movement stays matched to my aim.

Each dash still uses two presses. I am balancing the first press, its release, and the second press instead of minimizing every number.

How I kept the test clean

I built the base with actuation, RT D, and RT A only.

I kept the in-game setup I already used before moving from a mechanical keyboard to a Hall Effect keyboard. I set the actuation baseline first, used RT D to test the release, and used RT A to test the re-press. I left every other keyboard feature out of the baseline so each result stayed connected to one of those three settings.

Prove one selected move from standstill

I started with the smallest question: can one familiar GunZ move happen at all with this profile?

Actuation 1.50 mmRT A 1.50 mmRT D 0.55 mm
What I did and what I saw
  • Test: I used one selected move in the training room from standstill.
  • What stayed fixed: the move, keys, client, mouse, and game conditions.
  • What happened: the selected move could happen by itself.
  • What still failed: later inputs were very difficult to continue, so the profile did not yet support a movement chain.
What this result meant
  • What I learned: the keyboard could produce the required input states for one selected move.
  • What this did not prove: that I could release and re-press quickly enough for consecutive movement or a fight.
  • What I tested next: I kept actuation and RT D fixed, then reduced RT A to test repeated movement.

Continue moves while alone

I removed the enemy and aiming requirement so I could judge whether the movement itself would continue.

Actuation 1.50 mmRT A 1.50 → 0.60 mmRT D 0.55 mm
What I changed and what happened
  • What stayed fixed: actuation stayed at 1.50 mm and RT D stayed at 0.55 mm.
  • The only change: I lowered RT A from 1.50 mm to 0.60 mm.
  • What improved: I could perform moves and actions consecutively in training.
  • What this did not prove: there was still no enemy, crosshair tracking, or firing rhythm in this test.
Why the change helped
  • On the keyboard: 0.60 mm RT A required less downward re-press travel before the next tap was sent.
  • In GunZ: the later taps in the movement chain could arrive with less finger travel.
  • What I tested next: I kept that solo result and isolated the late-dash release with RT D.

Make the late-dash return clear

I tested whether the first tap of the return dash was releasing before its second tap.

Actuation 1.50 mmRT A 0.60 mmRT D 0.55 → 0.45 → 0.40 mm
What I tested and why
  • What I checked first: the opening dash remained normal when I checked familiar movement such as slash shot and half step.
  • What kept happening: the return dash was not silent often enough to show a release problem.
  • The only change: I lowered RT D from 0.55 mm to 0.45 mm, then to 0.40 mm.
  • What improved: at 0.40 mm, the release between the return dash's two taps became cleaner.
Why RT D was the setting to test
  • On the keyboard: lower RT D required less upward movement before the first tap was released.
  • In GunZ: the first tap of the return could clear before I pressed that direction for the second time.
  • What this did not prove: the repeated symptom pointed to RT D, but it did not prove that every failed late dash was caused by RT D.
  • What I tested next: I added an enemy and checked whether the movement stayed aligned with my aim.

Keep the slash shot dash and shot in rhythm

I added an enemy and kept my mouse and game settings fixed so the keyboard movement had to work inside the fight.

Actuation 1.50 mmRT A 0.60 → 0.35 mmRT D 0.40 mm
What failed in the fight
  • Starting point: the late-dash return was cleaner, and the solo movement chain already worked.
  • Repeated symptom: the next dash still arrived after the point where I expected to fire during slash shot.
  • Effect on aim: my crosshair and shot timing shifted, I missed, and I had to recover the chain before firing in rhythm again.
  • Only change: I lowered RT A from 0.60 mm toward 0.35 mm while RT D stayed at 0.40 mm.
  • Result: at 0.35 mm RT A, the dash and shot could stay in my intended rhythm while my crosshair placement remained accurate.
Why the enemy test changed the answer
  • Keyboard behavior: lower RT A reduced the downward re-press travel before the second or later repeated tap was sent.
  • GunZ meaning: the next dash could begin with less re-press travel and stay closer to my expected firing moment.
  • Why training was not enough: solo movement did not require me to track an enemy and fire at the same time.
  • Next decision: repeat the late-dash test because the faster re-press changed the rhythm of the full cycle.

Retest release after the rhythm changed

I returned to the late dash after the RT A change because a clean re-press does not guarantee that release still keeps up.

Actuation 1.50 mmRT A 0.35 mmRT D 0.40 → 0.15 mm
What the retest showed
  • What stayed fixed: RT A stayed at the 0.35 mm result that matched my firing rhythm.
  • What kept happening: the late dash exposed release delay again.
  • The only change: I lowered RT D from 0.40 mm toward 0.15 mm.
  • What improved: at 0.15 mm RT D, the return stayed clean enough for me to aim at the enemy with my current settings.
Why I returned to RT D
  • Why: the release problem repeated after the RT A change.
  • What did not decide it: I did not lower RT D merely because its number was higher than RT A.
  • What I learned: the repeated GunZ problem, not the numerical relationship between the values, decided which setting I tested.

Lower the actuation baseline

The earlier tune improved the cycle, but I still missed the late-dash timing often enough to investigate the first tap from full release.

Actuation 1.50 → 1.20 mmRT A reset nextRT D reset next
Why I changed actuation
  • Where I started: RT A and RT D had already improved the repeated release and re-press cycle.
  • What still failed: I continued to miss the late-dash timing often enough that the first tap also needed to be checked.
  • The only change: I lowered actuation from 1.50 mm to 1.20 mm.
  • What changed physically: the first tap after a full release required less downward travel.
What this does and does not prove
  • What I learned: 1.20 mm became the new personal actuation baseline I wanted to retest.
  • What this did not prove: that actuation caused every missed late dash.
  • Why I reset: changing actuation changed the baseline, so I did not keep the earlier tuned RT A and RT D values.
  • What I did next: I restored RT A and RT D to the saved restart values, then repeated the entire tuning sequence.

Return to a known base, then retune in training and real matches

After changing actuation, I returned to values I already knew I could clear, then rebuilt the result under the new 1.20 mm baseline.

Actuation 1.20 mmRT A 0.60 base → 0.80 training → 0.35 CW/TDMRT D 0.55 base → 0.30 training → 0.15 CW/TDM
How I reached that checkpoint
  • Known base: I restored RT A to 0.60 mm and RT D to 0.55 mm because I already knew I could clear movement from those values.
  • Training result: I tuned again under 1.20 mm actuation and reached 0.80 mm RT A and 0.30 mm RT D with good late dashes.
  • Real-match test: I took that result into Clan War first because it is the mode I actually prefer and the higher-skill pace leaves less room for errors. I used Team Deathmatch as another full-map validation.
  • Saved checkpoint: the Clan War and TDM pass ended at 0.35 mm RT A and 0.15 mm RT D.
Why I kept it
  • What the values represent: a combination that passed each stage of that FGunz test.
  • Why it remains useful: a later improvement does not invalidate the known base that made the next comparison possible.

Move from 1.20 mm to a 1.00 mm working base

I lowered the first-actuation distance to make the opening press more consistent without changing everything at once.

Actuation 1.20 → 1.00 mmRT A 0.20 mmRT D 0.15 mm
What I observed
  • Result: 1.00 mm worked and became another reliable saved base.
  • Remaining problem: higher-level matches and longer sessions could still demand more effort from the top of the key than I wanted.
Why I did not erase 1.20 mm
  • Comparison value: the 1.20 mm result still documents the earlier successful sequence.
  • Decision rule: I only continued because the same high-pace effort problem repeated over time.

Lower actuation again for sustained high-pace movement

I tested whether less travel from the top of the key could preserve the response I had at 1.00 mm while requiring less effort over longer sessions.

Actuation 1.00 → 0.60 mmRT A 0.15 mmRT D 0.25 mm
What improved
  • First press: the key reaches actuation with less downward travel from full release.
  • Consecutive movement: 0.15 mm RT A keeps the next input close to my intended pace.
  • Release control: 0.25 mm RT D gives me more intentional release margin than the earlier 0.15 mm checkpoint.
  • Longer sessions: the combination keeps the response I wanted while reducing the effort needed to maintain it.
What the current result means
  • It is a checkpoint: 0.60 / 0.15 / 0.25 is the latest combination that works for my hand, Wooting 60HE, and FreeStyle GunZ setup.
  • It is not a universal preset: the useful lesson is to preserve a known base, test one repeated problem, and keep the change only if it survives real play.

Latest FGunz result: 0.60 mm actuation · 0.15 mm RT A · 0.25 mm RT D. The recorded actuation path is 1.20 → 1.00 → 0.60 mm.

How I validate it

  1. Test the move alone. Training confirms that both presses of each dash, the release between them, and the return dash are clean.
  2. Use Clan War first, then Team Deathmatch as another validation. Clan War is my preferred higher-skill check. TDM adds another full-map test of movement directions, character control, aiming, and enemy pace.
  3. Do not use Hall Duel as the only proof. Most fights there stay mainly left and right, so they do not test the full range of motion.
  4. Check aim only after movement is clean. If aim still lands short or long, I check the mouse instead of automatically making Rapid Trigger faster.
  5. Keep it over time. I save the last repeatable result and change one setting only when the same problem repeats.

Repeat the tests in this order: familiar actuation → fluent RT A re-press → clean RT D release → RT A recheck → RT D recheck → training → Clan War → TDM validation → long-term check.

What I kept from earlier testing

The findings that still matter
  • I kept the one-change rule.
    • I wait for one problem to repeat, then change only the setting tied to that part of the key cycle.
    • I save a checkpoint first so a bad test does not erase the last good result.
  • I kept training and real matches as separate questions.
    • Training tells me whether I can repeat the movement by itself.
    • Clan War is my main real-match test because it is what I actually prefer and its higher-skill pace gives me less room for mistakes.
    • Team Deathmatch is another validation that the same movement still works while I use the map, control my character, aim, and react to enemy pace.
    • I do not use Hall Duel as the only final test because most fights there stay mainly left and right.
  • I kept the process more important than the final numbers.
    • My settings are personal checkpoints.
    • I separate documented keyboard behavior from what I personally observed in GunZ.

Evidence and limits

What I know · what I observed · what I am not claiming

What I observed in GunZ

  • I could keep a familiar working base and still improve it later.
    • RT D and RT A became easier to judge after I separated release from re-press.
    • My recorded actuation path is 1.20 mm to 1.00 mm to 0.60 mm.
    • My latest FGunz sequence stopped at 0.60 mm actuation, 0.15 mm RT A, and 0.25 mm RT D.
  • I needed to separate clean movement from aim.
    • Checking the mouse after the keyboard passed kept me from tuning a good movement result around a different problem.

Technical behavior supported by documentation

  • The documentation supports how the feature works.
    • Rapid Trigger follows key direction and travel instead of relying only on one fixed reset point.
    • Split sensitivity gives me separate upward/deactivate and downward/activate distances.
    • A smaller selected distance requires less physical travel, but it does not equal a fixed time saving.

What I am not claiming

  • My result is not a universal preset.
    • Another player, keyboard, switch, client, server, or finger motion can reach a different clean result.
  • My testing does not turn distance into time.
    • I am not claiming that a millimeter value equals a fixed number of milliseconds.
    • I am not claiming that every GunZ miss is caused by the keyboard or mouse.
    • My personal testing does not replace measured game or server data.
